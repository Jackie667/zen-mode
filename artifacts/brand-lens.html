The Mark

There is no separate symbol that outranks the wordmark — everything else in the system protects how it reads.

Anatomy, Not Decoration
  • Every letter is built from a single stroke of constant width, called "u" — it's also the exact height of the dot over the "i", so clear space can be measured by eye
  • The artboard is locked at a 3.09:1 proportion — the wordmark can never be stretched or squashed without breaking that ratio
  • Clear space is 1u minimum on all four sides, 2u wherever the layout allows — nothing enters that zone, not type, not another logo
  • Minimum sizes are specified precisely: 120px wide digital with the ® mark, 100px without, 24px for the standalone monogram, 30mm print
  • The mark is never rebuilt by typing the name in a typeface — the letterforms are drawn, not set, so only the supplied vector file is ever placed

Each misuse example is rendered live from the real mark rather than mocked up — so a supplier can see exactly what goes wrong, not just be told not to do it.

JSU's Take

"Most misuse pages show a crossed-out example and a caption. That still leaves room for 'well, my case is different.' Rendering the actual vector stretched, rotated and recoloured removes the argument — you're not describing the rule, you're showing the file breaking in real time."

  • Olive is reserved as a highlight and stays under roughly a tenth of any layout — it is never a text colour on a light background
  • Every pairing was measured against WCAG AA before it shipped: Ink on white passes at 17.61:1, Crimson on white at 5.52:1, Olive on ink at 6.65:1
  • Two combinations are flagged as failing and excluded from use entirely: Olive on white (2.65:1) and Crimson on ink for body text (3.19:1)
  • CMYK values are marked as converted, not press-proofed — anything over a few hundred print pieces gets a proof on the actual stock first, because crimson shifts on uncoated paper

A colour system is only as useful as the ratios nobody has to look up. When the accessibility maths is already done, "is this readable?" stops being a design debate.

JSU's Take

"Two colours that fail contrast on paper are more dangerous than ten that never made the palette at all — because they look fine until someone actually needs to read the text. Testing and excluding them up front is what makes the rest of the palette trustworthy."

02

Typography

A typeface that lives alongside a drawn wordmark without trying to imitate it.

Two Faces, One Job Each
  • Quicksand for headlines and display — rounded geometric terminals that rhyme with the mark's own curves, weights 500/600/700 only, never below 15px
  • Work Sans for body, tables and UI — a neutral grotesque that stays legible in dense specification tables and long-form copy, weights 300–600
  • A 1.25 type scale from a 16px base, headlines tracked tight at −1%, body left at zero
  • Sentence case everywhere, including buttons and navigation — the wordmark itself is lowercase, and shouting sits badly next to it
  • Chinese-language material pairs with Noto Sans SC at 1.05× the Latin size, so headings optically match rather than just matching in pixels

The typeface doesn't compete with the mark for attention — it does the reading work the mark was never meant to do.

JSU's Take

"A drawn wordmark can't set a sentence. The typeface pairing is what lets the same brand personality show up in a 40-word Instagram caption and a 4,000-word tenancy agreement without either one feeling like the wrong brand."

Brand Voice

Mid-market UK student housing settles most tone questions on its own: not luxury, not a party brand, not a corporate landlord.

Warm

Like a good flatmate, not a sales team. "You" and "we", never "residents" or "the customer" — but warm isn't matey. No forced slang, no emoji in headlines.

Straight

Name the number: rent, deposit, contract length, what's included, how far the walk is. Anything vague reads as a catch.

Useful

Every piece answers a real question someone had before they wrote in. If a sentence doesn't help someone decide, understand or do something, it gets cut.

Two People Read Everything We Write
  • An eighteen-year-old is choosing the room; a parent or guarantor is usually paying and signing for it — copy that speaks to only one of them alienates the other
  • "Five minutes from campus, bills included, £179 a week" reads as convenient to the student and competent to the parent — one line doing two jobs
  • British rental vocabulary is genuinely opaque to a largely international cohort — guarantor, tenancy, deposit protection, en suite — each term gets explained the first time it appears, in the same sentence, without reading like a glossary
  • Idioms and pop-culture references don't survive translation and often don't survive a year either — sentences stay under about twenty words

JSU's Take

"'We apologise for any inconvenience caused' isn't wrong, exactly — it's just designed to say nothing while sounding polite. The rewrite isn't more clever, it's just specific: a floor number, a time, an alternative. Specificity reads as care. Vagueness, even friendly vagueness, reads as a formula."

Photography Direction

Most student accommodation photography looks like a hotel brochure shot at midday. This should look like somewhere a person actually lives.

Warm, Precise, a Little Bit Charming
  • Soft daylight only, late afternoon, from a window — no flash, no HDR, no blue-hour lobby renders; shadows stay warm and are allowed to exist
  • Rooms are tidy but lived in: a folded throw, a half-finished coffee, plants — one deliberate sign of a person, never an empty room as the hero shot
  • Shot at 24–35mm and no wider — a 16mm lens makes a room look like a suite, and in a letting context that's a promise the building can't keep on move-in day
  • People are candid and mid-action, cast for the building actually there: eighteen to twenty-two, genuinely mixed nationalities, nobody looking at the camera
  • Detail shots at 50–85mm do the heaviest lifting on social — a labelled key fob, a kettle and two mugs, the pinboard; six of these tell the story better than one wide lobby shot
No Fisheye Room Shots No CGI as Photography No Stock Photos of Students No Sunset Skyline Stock No Motivational Text Over Images

The showroom and sleepover events from the Property Lens campaign only work because the finished room matches what people were shown — the photography direction has to hold that same line, or the trust breaks on move-in day.

JSU's Take

"If a shot could appear on any competitor's website with the logo swapped, it hasn't been made yet. The wide-angle hotel-brochure look isn't a style problem — it's a promise problem. It sells a room the student won't recognise when they arrive."

Systemising It

The part that makes a guideline usable instead of decorative: a specification for every surface the brand will actually touch.

One Spec Sheet per Channel
  • Social — exact export sizes for every placement across five platforms, from a 1080×1080 profile picture to a 1080×1920 Story, each with its own logo treatment and safe zone
  • Print & outdoor — a viewing-distance rule (x-height ≥ distance ÷ 250) scaling from a 30mm leaflet to a 12-metre 96-sheet billboard, so nobody has to guess how big is big enough
  • Digital — interface tokens derived from the mark's own geometry: fully rounded buttons, an 8/16/26/999px corner-radius scale, a 4–88px spacing scale, nothing between the steps
  • Merchandise — one colour, one logo, one placement per item, from a mug's 60mm wraparound wordmark to a 70mm embroidery minimum on apparel, below which the arches close up

None of this is exciting to write. It's exciting to use — it's the difference between a two-week email chain with a printer and a file that just works the first time.

JSU's Take

"The mark, the colours and the voice are the parts people notice. The format specs are the parts that make the campaign in Property Lens actually possible — because nobody had to reinvent the billboard layout from scratch three months after the Instagram templates were signed off."

What a Guideline Can't Do

"A brand guideline doesn't make the product good, and it doesn't make a bad decision defensible just because it's on-brand. What it does is remove the thousand small negotiations that would otherwise happen separately with every agency, printer and intern — so the only decisions left to argue about are the ones that actually matter."

— JSU

Where Judgement Still Has to Show Up

  • New formats and substrates. A building wrap, a partner lockup, an unusual material — none of those are decisions to make on deadline. Send the brief to the brand team and get an answer in writing.
  • Claims. Pricing, availability and inclusion claims have to be true when they publish and defensible afterwards — the voice guide does not override that check.
  • Physical proofing. Pantone references and CMYK conversions are the nearest match, not a guarantee — anything that matters gets a physical strike-off before the purchase order.
  • Annual review. A guideline that never gets revisited calcifies. This one carries a review date for the same reason the CGI renders in section 7 do.
